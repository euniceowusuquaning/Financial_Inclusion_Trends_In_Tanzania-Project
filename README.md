# Financial_Inclusion_Trends_In_Tanzania-Project
This dashboard, titled “Financial Inclusion Trends in Tanzania”, summarizes key insights about how people use mobile money, save, and participate in financial systems based on different demographics. Here’s what stands out:
1. Participants Overview
- Total participants: 7,092
- Gender split: 3,972 female (56%), 3,120 male (44%)

2. Mobile Money Usage
By Age Group:
- Young adults are the biggest users (3,734).
- Adults: 1,889
- Pensioners: 914
- Teenagers: 555    
*Insight: Mobile money adoption is highest among young adults.

By Gender:
- Female: 56%
- Male: 44%   
*Insight: Women slightly dominate mobile money usage.

Top Five Usage by Traders:
- Crop farmers lead (3,300)
- Non-traders: 2,710
Non-livestock traders, livestock traders, and agricultural traders follow with much smaller numbers.       
*Insight: Mobile money is strongly linked to farming activities.

3. Savings by Marital Status
- Married: 64%
- Single/never married: 17%
- Divorced: 10%
- Widowed: 9%   
*Insight: Married people save the most, possibly due to family responsibilities.

4. Education Levels
- Majority: Primary completed (3,446)
- Some primary: 1,046
- No formal education: 1,199
- University/higher: 708
- Some secondary: 467   
*Insight: Most users have basic education, which may affect how they interact with financial tools.

## Source  
The data used in this project is synthetic, aimed to be a representation of real-life data from kaggle.

**Insights**   
🔹 1. Mobile Money Usage by Age Group
- Young adults (3,734) are the most active users, more than double the adults (1,889).
- Teenagers (555) are the least engaged, likely due to limited financial independence and regulatory restrictions (e.g., needing ID to register).
- Pensioners (914) still show adoption, but lower than the working-age groups.

Insight:
- Mobile money has become the “default” financial tool for young adults, suggesting that financial inclusion programs targeting youth are working.
- However, low uptake among teenagers points to an untapped market that could be activated through education and youth-focused financial services.

🔹 2. Mobile Money Usage by Gender
- Women account for 56% of usage, while men represent 44%.
- This is significant because in many financial systems, women are often less represented.

Insight:
- Women’s slightly higher engagement could be linked to how mobile money supports household management, remittances, and small-scale trading where women dominate.
- It also shows mobile money is helping close the financial inclusion gender gap.

🔹 3. Mobile Money Usage by Traders
- Crop farmers (3,300) are the heaviest users, showing that agriculture is the main driver of mobile money transactions.
- Non-traders (2,710) also rely heavily on mobile money, possibly for personal payments, transfers, and bills.
- Livestock and agricultural traders are present but relatively small.

Insight:
- Agriculture is a cornerstone of mobile money adoption. Financial products designed for farmers (e.g., credit, insurance, and input purchases via mobile) would likely have strong uptake.
- The presence of non-traders highlights that mobile money is not just business-driven—it is also a household and personal financial tool.

🔹 4. Count of Savings by Marital Status
- Married individuals lead savings with 64%, followed by singles (17%) and divorced/widowed combined (19%).

Insight:
- Married individuals may save more due to family responsibilities, stability, or pooling of resources.
- Single people’s lower savings rates could reflect income challenges or lower financial responsibilities.
- Divorced/widowed groups saving less may point to financial vulnerability and the need for targeted financial empowerment programs.

🔹 5. Education Levels
- Primary completed (3,446) is the dominant education level among participants.
- Only 708 participants had university or higher education.
- A notable 1,199 had no formal education.

Insight:
- Financial inclusion is being driven largely by people with basic education, showing that mobile money platforms are accessible even to those with limited formal schooling.
- However, the 1,199 with no education highlight a need for financial literacy campaigns tailored in simple, non-technical language.
- This also suggests mobile money has the power to reach marginalized groups who may have been excluded from formal banking.

🔹 6. Overall Financial Inclusion Trends
- Mobile money is mainstream: It has bridged gaps where traditional banking may not have reached.
- Women and young adults are the leaders, showing how mobile money can be an equalizer in financial systems.
- Agriculture and farming communities are central, highlighting a direct link between rural livelihoods and financial technology.
- Savings behavior varies by marital status, with stability influencing financial planning.
- Education levels matter: While basic education is enough to adopt mobile money, more advanced financial products might require higher literacy.
